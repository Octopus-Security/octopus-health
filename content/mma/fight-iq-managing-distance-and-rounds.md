# Fight IQ: Managing Distance, Position and Rounds

"Fight IQ" is the ability to make good decisions under pressure: knowing where
to be, when to engage, and what to change. It can be learned, because most of
it is about a few recurring questions.

## 1. Which range do I want?

MMA has several ranges: kicking range, punching range, the clinch, and the
ground. Every fighter is stronger at some than others. The first question of
any game plan is: **where am I better than my opponent, and where are they
better than me?**

- A striker facing a wrestler wants to stay at range and keep their back off the cage.
- A wrestler facing a striker wants to close the distance safely — often behind strikes or by pinning the opponent against the cage.
- A fighter with a reach advantage wants the fight at the end of their long strikes; a shorter fighter wants to get inside quickly and stay there.

Fight IQ is imposing your range and refusing theirs.

## 2. Where am I in the cage?

- **The centre** gives you options: you can move in any direction.
- **Your back to the cage** takes options away: you cannot retreat, and the wall helps a wrestler hold you.
- **Cutting off the cage** — stepping laterally to block escape routes rather than following an opponent in circles — is how you push an opponent toward the wall.

A useful habit: after any exchange, ask "where is the cage?" and move back to the
centre if you are the one near it.

## 3. What are they showing me?

Opponents repeat patterns: the same entry, the same reaction to a feint, a
hand that drops after throwing a particular strike. Early in a fight, part of
the job is collecting information:

- Feint and watch the reaction.
- Notice what they do after they strike, and after they are hit.
- Notice which way they circle.

A pattern seen is an opening to plan for; a pattern you are showing is a
weakness for them to find.

## 4. How are the rounds going?

In most MMA competitions, judges score each round separately on the "10-point
must" system: the winner of a round usually gets 10 points and the loser 9 or
fewer. Two things follow:

- **Each round is its own contest.** Dominating one round does not bank extra points for a close later one.
- **Honest self-assessment between rounds matters.** Ask your corner: "Am I winning this round? What needs to change?"

Check the exact rules and judging criteria for your event — amateur rules and
different organisations vary.

## 5. Pacing

A fight plan that spends everything in the first round and has nothing left for
the third is not a plan. Know your conditioning (see the conditioning
breakdown), and match your pace to the number of rounds.

## Practising fight IQ

- **Positional sparring with a goal:** for example, one fighter must stay off the cage; the other must pin them to it. Control the intensity.
- **Film review:** watch your own sparring and note where the exchanges happened and who chose the range.
- **Between-round questions:** after each sparring round, answer three questions — where did it happen, who controlled it, what should change next round?

## Safety notes

- Controlled, goal-based sparring teaches decision-making with far fewer impacts than all-out sparring. Most learning happens below full intensity.
- Train and spar under a qualified coach, with proper protective equipment (mouthguard, appropriate gloves, and groin protection).
- Any symptoms of concussion mean stopping and getting medically assessed. A clear head is part of fight IQ.
