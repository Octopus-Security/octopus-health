# Conditioning: Built for the Five-Minute Round

## What a round demands

A professional MMA round lasts five minutes with one minute of rest between
rounds. Inside those five minutes, effort is anything but steady: bursts of
explosive striking or scrambling, then grinding clinch or ground work, then
brief lulls. Conditioning for it has to cover two needs:

1. **Repeatable bursts** — the ability to explode again and again.
2. **Recovery between bursts** — getting your breath back in seconds, and recovering in the one-minute rest.

## Why the aerobic base comes first

The recovery between bursts is powered mostly by the aerobic system. A fighter
with a poor aerobic base can be explosive once or twice and then fades,
because they cannot recover quickly enough between efforts. That is why many
fight camps build steady aerobic work (see *Zone 2 Aerobic Base* in the workouts
folder) before and alongside hard intervals. The engine comes first; the
top speed is built on it.

## Why "just spar more" is not enough

Sparring develops skill and specific fitness, but hard sparring is also the
highest-injury part of training, and you cannot control its intensity
precisely. Separate conditioning lets you get fitter without taking more
impacts.

## A round-simulation session

Once you have a base, this session mimics the structure of a fight. Start with
three rounds and work up.

**Warm-up:** 10 minutes — easy rope, dynamic mobility, a few short accelerations.

**Each round (5 minutes):** repeat this 1-minute cycle five times:
- 20 seconds hard: bag punches in combinations, or an air bike sprint
- 10 seconds: 3–5 sprawls or burpees
- 30 seconds moderate: pad or shadow footwork, or grappling movements (shrimps, technical stand-ups)

**Rest:** 1 minute. Stand, breathe slowly through the nose, and try to bring
your breathing under control — that recovery is part of the training.

**Cool-down:** 5–10 minutes easy movement, then stretching.

Record how many rounds you completed and how your last round compared to your
first. The goal is for the last round to look like the first.

## Scheduling

- Put hard conditioning on a different day from hard sparring where possible.
- Keep one or two sessions a week easy (aerobic), and no more than two or three genuinely hard conditioning sessions.
- Reduce conditioning volume in the final week or so before a competition so you arrive fresh — sharp, not exhausted.

## Safety notes

- Build up gradually. Jumping straight into multi-round high-intensity sessions is a common route to overuse injury and excessive fatigue.
- Stop if you have chest pain, faintness, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort, and get medically assessed.
- Hydrate and refuel after hard sessions (see the hydration and plate pieces in the nutrition folder).
- If you have a heart condition or other health concerns, get medical clearance before high-intensity interval training.
