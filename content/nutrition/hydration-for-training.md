# Hydration for Training

Needs vary a great deal with body size, climate, and how much you sweat, so
there is no single correct daily amount. The useful skill is learning to read
your own signals.

## Everyday: three simple checks

1. **Thirst.** For most healthy people in everyday life, drinking when thirsty is a reasonable default.
2. **Urine colour.** Pale straw-coloured is a good sign. Consistently dark yellow suggests you need more. (Some vitamins and foods change the colour, so use it as a trend.)
3. **Regularity.** Build habits instead of forcing large amounts at once: a glass with each meal, a bottle at your desk, a bottle in your training bag.

## Around training

- **Before:** start sessions already hydrated — drink normally through the day rather than downing a large amount just before training.
- **During:** for sessions under about an hour in moderate conditions, water is usually enough. For long sessions, hot rooms, or multiple sessions a day, a drink containing sodium (and some carbohydrate) helps replace what sweat takes out.
- **After:** weigh yourself before and after a hard session a few times, in similar clothing and after towelling off. The difference is mostly fluid lost as sweat — roughly one kilogram of loss is about one litre of fluid. It tells you how much you lose in *your* sessions, so you can plan to replace it over the following hours with fluids and salted food.

## Signs you are behind

Headache, unusual fatigue, dizziness on standing, dark urine, and performance
that drops off faster than usual. Muscle cramps have several causes, but heavy
sweating without replacing sodium is a common one.

## More is not always better

Drinking far more water than you lose, especially plain water over long
endurance events, can dilute blood sodium to dangerous levels (hyponatraemia).
Symptoms include nausea, headache, confusion and swelling — they can resemble
dehydration, which is what makes it dangerous. Drink to thirst and to your
measured losses, not to a heroic target.

## A word on weight cutting

Deliberate dehydration to make a weight class is a serious medical risk, and
combat-sports bodies have introduced rule changes in response to athlete
deaths associated with extreme cuts. It is not covered here as a technique. If
you compete with weigh-ins, work with a qualified sports dietitian and your
coach, and choose a division you can make without drastic dehydration.

## When to get help

Seek medical care for confusion, fainting, a racing heart that does not settle,
or being unable to keep fluids down.
