# Calisthenics — Progressions and Routines

Bodyweight training gets harder by changing the *exercise*, not by adding
plates. Each movement family below is a **ladder**: a sequence of rungs from
easiest to hardest. You work on one rung until you meet its standard, then move
up to the next.

## Equipment this section assumes

Only three pieces of kit, plus the floor and a wall:

- **Pull-up bar.** A doorway, wall-mounted, or rack bar.
- **Dip bars.** A dip station or parallel bars.
- **Push-up handles.** **Assumption:** these are treated as *parallettes-style* handles: low, stable, and solid enough to support full bodyweight in an L-sit, crow or planche lean, not just a push-up. If yours rock, rotate or are built only for push-ups, use them for push-ups only and do the support skills on the dip bars instead.

Anything else is marked **optional**: a chair or box, a resistance band, a
backpack with books for added load, a cushion. Nothing in the core programme
requires rings, weights or a gym.

**Check your bar before you trust it.** A doorway bar is fine for hanging and
pulling but may not allow muscle-ups, full-range levers or kipping. There
often isn't enough clearance, and some doorway bars can lever off the frame if
you pull outward or get above the bar. A freestanding dip station can tip if you
lean hard to one side or pull on it from underneath. Each ladder calls out
where this matters.

## How progression works

### The standards

These ladders use one convention throughout:

- **Dynamic exercises** (reps): move up when you can do **3 sets of the target reps with clean form**, in the same session, with normal rest. Most rungs use a target of 8–12. Harder skill-strength rungs use a lower target (3–6).
- **Holds** (seconds): move up when you can do **3 holds of the target time** with clean form.
- **Per-side exercises** (archer, one-leg): the standard applies to **each side**, and to your weaker side first.

These are coaching conventions, chosen so a rung is solid before you leave it.
They are not measured physiological thresholds.

### What "clean form" means here

A rep counts only if:

- it goes through the **full range** stated for that rung;
- you **control the lowering**, with no dropping;
- you **don't kip, swing or bounce**, unless the rung says to;
- your **body position holds**: no sagging hips, no shrugged shoulders at the bottom of a hang or dip, no elbows flaring beyond what the rung describes.

The last reps of a set can be slower. They cannot be different.

### Moving up

When you meet a standard, start the next rung. Don't abandon the old one
overnight. For a session or two, do the new rung first while fresh and
finish with a set of the old one. Expect your reps to drop sharply on the new
rung. That is normal: the new rung is supposed to be harder.

### Regressing

Drop back a rung (or use that rung's listed regression) when:

- you can't get **3 clean reps** (or a clean 10-second hold) on a set;
- form breaks on **the first rep** rather than the last;
- a movement causes **pain**. That means joint or tendon pain, sharp or pinching. Ordinary muscular effort is not pain.

Regressing is not failure. Building volume on an easier rung is how you earn
the harder one.

### Stalls and deloads

- **Stalled for three sessions or more** on the same rung with no rep or hold-time gain: check sleep and food first. Then try a "split" of the rung, such as negatives of it, or a pause at the hardest point.
- **Deload** every 4–8 weeks, or sooner if joints ache, motivation drops, or performance falls for several sessions. For one week, keep the same exercises but do about half the sets. Stop every set well short of failure. Skip max-effort attempts and new skills.
- Come back from a deload at the same rungs, not harder ones.

## Safety, across every ladder

- **Tendons adapt more slowly than muscles.** Elbows, wrists and shoulders often lag behind your strength. Add volume gradually, especially on dips, straight-arm work (levers, planche, L-sit) and anything new.
- **Straight-arm strength needs its own ramp-up.** Start straight-arm skills with short holds, a few sets, and no more than two or three sessions a week. Stop before form fails. Straight-arm holds to failure are how elbows and biceps tendons get irritated.
- **Warm up the joints you are about to load**: wrist circles before handles and handstands, scapular pulls before hanging, support holds before dips.
- **Wrists:** the handles let you keep your wrists straight (neutral) for push-ups and supports. That's easier on them than a flat palm on the floor. Handstand work on the floor still loads the wrists in extension, so warm them up.
- **Pain rule:** sharp, pinching or joint pain means stop that exercise for the day and regress next time. Pain that persists or worsens across sessions is for a physiotherapist or sports-medicine clinician, not for more stretching.
- **Falls:** practise the bail from handstands and levers before you need it. Clear the space under bars and walls.
